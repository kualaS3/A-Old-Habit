## Iteration 5

This one tries to be a near-perfect simulation of a 1-on-1 game (player against dealer) and fixes the split odds, which were the one thing iteration 3 had wrong (well, it was simplified). And uh.. theres a lot of changes to this one since iteration 4 so bear with me

- **Split odds, finally near-exact.** The two hands share one shrinking shoe and one dealer hand, so they cannot be valued independently (obviously) and that was the whole approximation. The exact formulation is a single joint recursion over both hands and the dealer, but its state space explodes with resplits: 389,000 states at one split, 27,000,000+ at two, out of memory at three. So the live engine is a sequential decomposition instead, in the order the game actually plays the hands. The hand being split is played to maximise the total EV of every hand: when it stands, doubles or busts, the hands still to come are valued by the tail, which plays each of them to maximise its own EV and returns the expected dealer distribution after it. This means the first hand's decisions do account for what its cards do to the second hand and to the dealer. The one thing the later hands do not account for is the reverse: a later hand's draws also move the dealer distribution the earlier hands are settled against, and its decisions are not re-optimised for that. That effect is second-order, where a card removed moves the earlier hands' EV by about a hundredth, and it only flips a later hand's decision when that decision is already within a hundredth of a tie. At one split the decomposition agrees with the joint exactly on every spot checked (8,8 vs 6: both +0.3420 on a full deck, A,A vs 5: both +0.7322, 8,8 vs 10: both −0.4521), so the split EV is exact up to well under a hundredth, far inside simulation noise. One honest limit remains: the SPLIT row carries only its combined EV now (the per-hand win/push/lose split is not tracked) because the hands' outcomes are correlated through the one dealer hand and cannot be summarised per hand. And the per-hand HIT/STAND odds shown while you are playing a split hand still value that hand alone.

- **The worst case, and why split odds need so much attention.** Take 8,8 against a ten. The old approximation said splitting is worth −0.453 per unit, the best of a bad set (stand −0.543, hit −0.507), so the trainer would tell you to split. And for a single split it was right: the exact value is −0.4521. But the real game allows resplits, and against a ten that changes everything: every time you split and draw another 8, you face the same bad spot again, and each additional hand is another loser against a ten. The true game, simulated over 60,000 rounds a spot, comes out at −0.633, which is a full 0.18 worse than the approximation's number. So the approximation didn't just misprice the split, it understated how bad the spot is by almost a fifth of a bet, because it treated the two hands as if each got its own dealer and its own shoe, and it could never see that splitting against a ten keeps manufacturing more losing hands. That is why the split needed the joint recursion (or the decomposition) instead of the independent-hands shortcut: the hands are not independent, and the difference is largest exactly where the decision matters most.

- **Why C, and why the decomposition.** The split EV needs a recursion over the composition of the unseen cards, and the number of positions that recursion visits is the whole problem. A single hand with no split is about 1,300 positions and 0.03 seconds. That is the scale a split has to beat, and every extra hand multiplies it. In pure Python each position costs so much (dictionaries, tuples, function calls) that the joint recursion took 58 seconds and 1.6 million cached states for one spot with resplitting switched off, and was killed for memory with it on. The Python decomposition was worse still, timing out past 280 seconds without finishing one spot. The same maths in C is plain integer arrays and hash tables, so each position costs almost nothing, but C does not reduce how many positions there are, and that count is what explodes with resplits. The table below is the measured story, for a full deck, 8,8 against a 6 (49 unseen cards), on this machine (6GB).

  | Engine | Max splits | Positions (states) | Memory (peak) | Time | 8,8 vs 6 EV |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Python joint | 1 | 1.6M | not recorded | 58s | not recorded |
  | Python decomposition | 1 | not recorded | - | >280s, timed out | - |
  | C joint (exact) | 1 | 389k joint, 525k dealer | 191MB | 1.05s | +0.3420 |
  | C joint (exact) | 2 | 27.1M joint, 7.0M dealer | 3.3GB | 72s | +0.3848 |
  | C joint (exact) | 3 | >100M (est.) | OOM (>6GB) | did not finish | - |
  | C decomposition (live) | 1 | 231k total | 325MB | 2.71s | +0.3420 |
  | C decomposition (live) | 2 | 1.78M total | 957MB | 17.8s | +0.3598 |
  | C decomposition (live) | 3 | - | OOM | 66s to fail | - |
  | MC, true game (60k rounds) | 3 | - | - | - | +0.393 |

  Three things to read out of that table. First, C is necessary because Python is ~50x too slow per position: the joint goes from 58 seconds to 1 second, and the decomposition from timing out to under 3 seconds. Second, the decomposition is the live engine not because it is the fastest at one split. It isn't. The joint is faster and lighter there (1.05s and 191MB against 2.71s and 325MB, because the decomposition's memo values are wide and it asks the dealer for a distribution far more often). It is the live engine because it is the only engine that survives a second split: 1.78 million states and 18 seconds, where the joint is already at 27 million states, 72 seconds and 3.3GB. It grows about 8x per added split instead of about 70x, which buys it one extra split of headroom. Third, neither engine survives three splits: both run out of memory. That is the wall the whole iteration runs into, and it is why the default split cap is one (see below). The C code lives in `blackjack/split_exact.c`, with both engines in the one file. The decomposition is the live entry point (`split_ev_decomp`) and the joint is the verification tool (`split_ev_exact`), loaded through `blackjack/split_exact.py`. Build it with `python build.py` (needs gcc or cc). The shared library it produces (`_split_exact.so`) is platform-specific and is not committed, so each checkout builds its own. Without it, split odds raise a clear error telling you to build it, never a silent fallback to a wrong number.

- **A significant limitation: one split, no resplits, by default.** `Rules.max_splits` defaults to 1. That means the game, the odds and the CLI all allow exactly one split per round: split a pair once, and the resulting hands can never be split again, even if they pair up. This is a real limitation, not a simplification. Real tables allow resplitting (usually up to three splits, four hands), and resplits are worth real money: on 8,8 vs 6 the exact value rises from +0.3420 at one split to +0.3848 at two, and the simulated true game at three splits is +0.393, so the trainer understates the value of a good split by about 0.05. And it cuts the other way too: on 8,8 vs 10 the one-split value is −0.4521 while the true game plays out near −0.633, so the trainer makes a bad split look 0.18 better than it is. Capping at one split flattens the value of splitting in both directions. The reason is the table above: neither engine can compute three splits, and at two splits the decomposition's value is already 0.025 low against the joint (+0.3598 vs +0.3848), so the resplit numbers are less certain than the one-split numbers, which match the joint exactly. The default is therefore the case the engine can stand behind exactly, in a few seconds. Raise `max_splits` in config if you want resplits. The game, the odds and the CLI all follow, knowing the numbers beyond one split are slower and, at three, unavailable. One more consequence of the same cap: the decomposition stands a restricted pair of split aces rather than resplitting it (the joint follows the game and resplits them), which only matters when the cap is raised above one. With the default cap there is no resplit at all, so it never arises.

- **The archived Python version.** The pure-Python decomposition that iteration 5 shipped first lives at `blackjack/archive/split_sequential.py`, kept to show the attempt. It is the same algorithm as the live C engine, in Python, and it is not imported anywhere and not used at runtime. On a 13-card pool it takes about a second a spot and reproduces the C engine's values exactly (8,8 vs 6: +0.3522 at one split, +0.3868 at three, A,A vs 5: +0.6709, 8,8 vs 10: −0.4364 at one split, −0.4280 at three). On a full deck it takes minutes a spot. One spot did not finish in 280 seconds, which is why it was ported to C. Run it as a demo with `python -m blackjack.archive.split_sequential`.

- **The CLI computes the split on demand.** The live odds table never computes the split EV: the C decomposition takes a few seconds on a full shoe, so it would make every pair decision feel sluggish. Press `o` on a pair and it computes the split EV and shows the best action with splitting included. (The pure-Python version took minutes, which is why it is archived.)

- **Shoes, 1 to 8 decks** (`Rules.num_decks`, default 1), with a **cut card** in config. The cut is the number of cards dealt before the reshuffle: an exact position (`Rules.cut_cards`, validated to leave at least ten cards), or the realistic version, a fraction of the shoe, 3/4, 2/3 or 1/2 (`Rules.cut_fraction`, default 3/4), with the real cut landing within five cards either side, re-rolled on every shuffle. A round in progress always finishes before the reshuffle. The **burn card** (`Rules.burn_card`, on by default) removes the top card before the deal. It is never drawn, never counted, and never in the odds, because in the real game it can never be drawn.
- **Insurance and even money** (`Rules.insurance_allowed`, on by default). Against a dealer ace, insurance costs half your bet and pays 2:1 if the dealer has blackjack. Its EV is 3p − 1, where p is the tens share of the unseen cards, so about −0.077 on a full shoe: a sucker bet, shown with its EV before you decide. With a blackjack against a dealer ace you are offered even money instead: 1:1 now, against 1.5 × (1 − p) if you decline. At 3:2 declining is worth about 1.04, so even money is the worse deal. At 6:5 declining is worth about 0.83, so even money is the better deal. The game settles both correctly either way.
- **The money is in config now.** Starting balance $1,000 (`Rules.starting_balance`), table minimum $10 and maximum $1,000 (`Rules.min_bet`, `Rules.max_bet`). The CLI enforces them at the bet prompt.
- **6:5 blackjack** is a third payout option (`Rules.blackjack_payout` now allows (3,2), (2,1) or (6,5)). A natural pays 1.2 on the stake, and the even-money maths above follows it.
- **Rule toggles** (`Rules.double_after_split`, `Rules.resplit_aces`, `Rules.hit_split_aces`, all defaulting to the usual table): double after split on, resplitting aces on (up to the split cap), split aces take exactly one card and cannot be hit. Turn any of them off and the game, the odds and the CLI all follow. A split hand that cannot double is never offered DOUBLE, and a split-ace pair that cannot resplit is never offered SPLIT.

### Painfully large expansionary ideas, inspired by the split problem

**Valuing a decision for the whole shoe, not just this round.** The split problem taught us that a small-sounding extension, "just value both hands together", can explode into millions of states. This one is the same pattern, worse. Right now every decision is valued for this round only, given the shoe as it is. Hit on 16 (say 7♣ 9♠) and the engine averages over every card you could draw, and the card you draw is gone: the dealer plays from what is left, and so does your second hit. What it does not count is the future. Draw a ten on that 16 and you bust. That is in the number, but you have also taken a ten out of the shoe, and tens are good for the player (more blackjacks, more dealer busts), so every hand until the reshuffle is a touch worse. The engine ignores that. To count it, you would have to value the decision against every hand you might ever be dealt from this shoe until the cut card, every possible future deal, every future decision, every future composition. That is the split problem again, but over a whole shoe of rounds instead of one round of hands, so it is not built. The effect is real, but it belongs to card counting, which handles it separately through bet sizing, not through the hit and stand maths. Noted here so the idea is on the record, and the reason it stays an idea.
