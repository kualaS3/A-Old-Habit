## Iteration 6b

Iterations 1 to 5 answer one question: what is the best move for this hand, right now. This iteration answers a different one: if you play that way for a thousand rounds, what happens to your money.

The simulator plays the game by itself. It deals shoe after shoe, follows basic strategy for every decision, sizes each bet by the Kelly rule, and records the bankroll after every round. It does not call the exact engine while it plays. That would take seconds on a split hand, and a thousand round session would take days. Basic strategy is the fixed table of best moves, and the exact engine is what proves the table is right. The simulator trusts the table and spends its time on the thing the engine cannot do: playing a lot of rounds.

It reports three numbers. Risk of ruin is how often the bankroll hits zero before the session ends. Max drawdown is the worst fall from a peak. Growth is where the bankroll ends up on average. It runs every session twice, once with Kelly sizing and once with flat betting, so the Kelly numbers have something to beat. A sizing rule is only interesting if it beats betting the same amount every hand.

Two warnings. The ruin numbers come from simulation, not from a formula. The textbook formula assumes every round has the same edge and the same variance. Real rounds do not, because the edge moves with the count. The formula is in here as a check on the simulator, not as the answer. And full Kelly is here because it is the theory, not because it is the advice. In practice it swings hard enough that most people bet a fraction of it. That fraction is a setting in the config, and the simulator is the way to see what each fraction costs you in growth and saves you in drawdown.

- **Basic strategy, and the three cells the engine corrected.** The table is in `blackjack/basic_strategy.py`, written out as data for the standard game this project plays: multi-deck, dealer stands on soft 17, double after split, late surrender, blackjack pays 3:2. A lookup is instant, so the simulator spends its time playing rounds. The table ignores the composition of the hand (a 16 is a 16 whether it is 10+6 or 9+7), and a few cells are within a hundredth of a tie, so the exact engine on a particular composition can disagree with it there. The verification (`python verify_basic_strategy.py`) generates the table from the exact engine and reports every cell where they differ, and it caught three real things. First, the generator was not asking the engine for split odds at all (analyze() only computes the split when the caller asks for it), so every pair cell came out as the best non-split action. That was a bug in the generator, not the table, and fixing it made the pair cells agree. Second, soft 19 against a 6: the published table doubles it, but on a multi-deck S17 shoe standing beats doubling by about a hundredth (+0.4939 against +0.4803), and doubling only wins on H17 (+0.4621 against +0.4522) or on one deck (by two ten-thousandths). The doubling play is the H17 play, not the S17 one, so this table stands on soft 19 against a 6. Third, a pair of 8s against an ace: the table first had surrender, but the split is worth -0.3852 on a six-deck shoe against -0.5 for surrendering, and the two C engines agree on that number, so 8s are split against an ace too. The remaining disagreements after those fixes are the composition ties: hard 12 against a 4, where the engine says hit by 0.0007 on the representative hand (10+2) but stand by 0.0074 on 9+3, and standing is right on average across the compositions of 12, so the table stands. The low pairs (2s to 6s) were checked against the split premiums warmed in 6a rather than by the live decomposition, because the decomposition takes 30-45 seconds per low-pair cell on six decks and a full table check runs over half an hour. The targeted check (hard and soft rows, the fast pair ranks, and the low pairs by premium) found nothing else.

- **The Illustrious 18, as a byproduct of the EV.** The 18 most valuable deviations from basic strategy (Schlesinger) are not programmed in anywhere. They fall out of the exact engine: for each of the 18 plays, the EV of the deviation is valued against the EV of the basic strategy play at each true count, and the count where the deviation takes over is the index. The check samples a few shoe compositions at each integer true count, deals the entry's hand from each one, values both actions exactly, averages the EV difference, and interpolates the crossing. The measured flips against the published indices, six decks, S17, double after split, late surrender, 3:2:

  | Play | Published index | Measured flip |
  |---|---|---|
  | Insurance | +3 | +4.3 |
  | 16 vs 10, stand | 0 | +1.4 |
  | 15 vs 10, stand | +4 | +4.4 |
  | 10,10 vs 5, split | +5 | +5.2 |
  | 10,10 vs 6, split | +4 | +4.7 |
  | 10 vs 10, double | +4 | +5.0 |
  | 12 vs 3, stand | +2 | +2.4 |
  | 12 vs 2, stand | +3 | +4.0 |
  | 11 vs A, double | +1 | +1.3 |
  | 9 vs 2, double | +1 | +1.1 |
  | 10 vs A, double | +4 | +4.0 |
  | 9 vs 7, double | +3 | +3.1 |
  | 16 vs 9, stand | +5 | +4.0 |
  | 13 vs 2, stand | -1 | -1.2 |
  | 12 vs 4, stand | 0 | +0.1 |
  | 12 vs 5, stand | -2 | -1.9 |
  | 12 vs 6, stand | -1 | -1.3 |

  13 of the 18 land within 1 true count of the published index, and the worst miss is 1.4 (16 vs 10). That scatter is honest. Only a few compositions were sampled per count, and the EV curves are very flat near the flip, so a small sampling wobble moves the crossing by a count. The published indices are also integers from a slightly different true-count convention and slightly different rules. None of the 18 is missing or far off: every deviation falls out of the EV in the right direction at about the published count. One case the engine knows more than the index: on 16 vs 10 with late surrender, surrendering at -0.5 beats both hitting and standing at low counts, so the published 0 is the stand-vs-hit flip for a no-surrender game.

- **The Kelly policy.** The bet is `kelly_fraction * edge(true_count) / variance(true_count) * bankroll`, clamped to the table minimum and maximum and to whole dollars. The edge comes from the 6a curve (a lookup by true count, interpolated linearly between bucket centres, holding the nearest end outside the measured range), and the variance is the 6a total round variance: the variance of the round before the cards are dealt, which is the mean conditional variance given the dealt hand plus the spread of the hand EVs across deals. A negative edge bets the table minimum. The fraction is `Rules.kelly_fraction`, half Kelly (0.5) by default. The policy is in `blackjack/kelly.py`, with `FlatBetPolicy` as the baseline that bets the same amount every hand.

  Correction: the variance this iteration first sized bets with was the wrong one. The 6a curve carried the mean conditional variance, about 1.05 at a true count of 0. The Kelly formula needs the total round variance, about 1.29, because the bet is placed before the cards are dealt. The bets were therefore about 23% larger than true Kelly, and the half-Kelly default behaved like about 0.61 Kelly. The curve now carries the total variance, and the sample run below was rerun with the corrected bets. The simulator numbers stand as measured outcomes of the bets actually placed in each run.

- **The simulator.** `blackjack/simulator.py` drives the existing `Round`, `Shoe` and `CardCounter` with basic strategy decisions and a bet policy. It never calls the exact engine. A session is configured by `SessionConfig`: the number of rounds, an optional cap on shoes, and an optional win goal or stop loss (deltas from the starting bankroll). Ruin is a bankroll under the table minimum, which cannot place the next bet. The count drives two decisions: the bet is sized from the true count at the start of the round, before any cards are dealt, and insurance and even money are taken only at a true count of +3 or better, the Hi-Lo index where the dealer is likely enough to have blackjack for the side bet to pay. A split or a double the bankroll cannot cover falls back to the next best move (double becomes hit, split becomes the hand played as its total). `run_sessions` runs a batch of seeded sessions and aggregates them: the risk of ruin, the mean growth, the mean max drawdown and the mean ending bankroll. The textbook analytic risk of ruin (`analytic_risk_of_ruin`) is the diffusion approximation for a fixed bet and a constant edge and variance, and it is in here as a cross-check, not the answer.

  The sample run, 50 sessions of 2000 rounds on a $10,000 bankroll, half Kelly against flat $10, six decks, S17, double after split, late surrender, 3:2, seed 20261111:

  | Policy | Risk of ruin | Mean growth | Mean max drawdown | Mean ending bankroll |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Kelly (half) | 0.000 | +0.019 | $1023.3 | $10190.8 |
  | Flat $10 | 0.000 | +0.001 | $605.1 | $10005.4 |

  Half Kelly grows about 1.9% over 2000 rounds where flat betting is flat, because it puts more money out when the count says the edge is positive, and it pays for that with about 1.7 times the drawdown ($1023 against $605). Neither ruins from $10,000 in 2000 rounds, which is not a surprise at these bet sizes. The ruin numbers would separate at a smaller bankroll or a longer session. The analytic risk of ruin at the curve mean edge (-0.0013) and variance (1.289), flat $10 bet, is 1.000 (cross-check only). That is the long-run limit for a negative mean edge: a negative-edge game ruins you eventually, but 2000 rounds is not eventually, so the simulation says 0.000 while the formula says 1.000, and both are right about different horizons. The formula also assumes a fixed bet and a constant edge, and neither holds here, which is why it is a check and not the answer.

- **How to run it.** Build the C helper first (`python build.py`, needs gcc or cc) -- the simulator itself does not need it, but the basic strategy verification does. Run a batch with `python run_simulation.py --bankroll 10000 --rounds 2000 --sessions 50 --seed 20261111` (about a minute). Verify the table against the exact engine with `python verify_basic_strategy.py --decks 6` (over half an hour, most of it the low-pair cells: the decomposition takes 30-45 seconds per low-pair cell on six decks). The tests run with `pytest tests/` (149 passed, 2 skipped, about 55 seconds). The skipped tests are the 6a TC-0 anchor and the every-bucket total-variance check, both gated behind AOH_SLOW=1.
